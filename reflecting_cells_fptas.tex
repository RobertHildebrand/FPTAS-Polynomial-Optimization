\begin{abstract}
We give a short proof of a fully polynomial-time range-relative approximation scheme for polynomial optimization over the integer points of a bounded rational polyhedron in fixed dimension. Boundedness supplies a finite slack decomposition and a finite integer objective range. The proof combines the reflecting-set decomposition of Cook, Hartmann, Kannan, and McDiarmid with Markov's polynomial derivative inequality and uses one integer linear feasibility query per refined cell. The running time is polynomial in the input encoding size, the numerical degree, and the reciprocal accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and main result}

Let $P=\{\mathbf{x}\in\R^d:A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf b\}$ be a bounded rational polyhedron, with $A\in\Q^{m\times d}$ and $\mathbf b\in\Q^m$, and let $f\in\Z[x_1,\ldots,x_d]$. We study
\begin{equation}\label{eq:problem}
 \begin{aligned}
 \min\quad & f(\mathbf{x})\\
 \text{s.t.}\quad & A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf b,\\
 & \mathbf{x}\in\Z^d.
 \end{aligned}
\end{equation}
When $P\cap\Z^d\ne\varnothing$, choose minimizers and maximizers $\mathbf{x}_{\min},\mathbf{x}_{\max}$ and write $f_{\min}=f(\mathbf{x}_{\min})$ and $f_{\max}=f(\mathbf{x}_{\max})$.

We use $d$ for dimension, $D$ for total degree, and $\mathbf{x}_{\eps}$ for the returned point, following~\cite{DHKW08}. The range-relative guarantee is their weak approximation guarantee, stated here for minimization. It is invariant under translation of the objective. It is different from a multiplicative approximation to a nonnegative minimum.

De Loera, Hemmecke, K\"oppe, and Weismantel established fully polynomial approximation schemes based on short rational generating functions, including nonnegative polynomial maximization and range-relative approximation of arbitrary polynomial objectives in fixed dimension \cite{DHKW06,DHKW08}. Del Pia gives a geometric range-relative approximation algorithm for bounded mixed-integer quadratic programs with fixed quadratic rank and fixed number of integer variables \cite{DelPia}. We give an alternative construction for the bounded, pure-integer problem. The purpose is to obtain a short proof using linear feasibility, with explicit dependence on the encoding length.

\emph{The common geometric ingredient is a decomposition by relative slack scales.} The dyadic reflecting sets of Cook--Hartmann--Kannan--McDiarmid (CHKM) are central to their integer-hull analysis~\cite{CHKM}. Symmetric displacement covers likewise underpin the quadratic and cubic algorithms of Ari and Hildebrand~\cite{AHIQP,AH}. In parallel work on exact integer quadratic programming, Wei explicitly uses dyadic grouping of constraint slacks to obtain regions supporting two-sided feasible moves~\cite[Section~1.1]{Wei}. These uses suggest that relative-slack decompositions are a useful common tool for polynomial integer optimization: they convert proximity measured against the constraints into feasible algebraic comparisons, without reference to objective coefficients.

Here refinement strengthens one-step reflection to a long feasible arithmetic progression. Restricting a degree-$D$ polynomial to that progression and applying Markov's polynomial derivative inequality~\cite[Theorem~5.1.8]{BorweinErdelyi} then yields an objective-independent representative set that approximates both integer extrema; see Proposition~\ref{prop:extrema}. Thus the geometric cover serves all polynomial objectives of the prescribed degree simultaneously. The essential feature is multiplicative control of positive slacks, not the choice of base two: Remark~\ref{rem:slack-characterization} makes the connection to extrapolation exact. Piazzon and Vianello~\cite[Propositions~1--2]{PV} obtain range-relative approximation from norming meshes by choosing arbitrary feasible representatives in sufficiently small boxes. Our refined reflecting cells instead supply feasible integer progressions, so the error is measured against the integer objective range.

Markov's inequality is essential to the polynomial cell bound: it controls variation relative to the integer objective range, so the required refinement depends only on degree and accuracy. A coefficient-based Lipschitz estimate alone could require refinement exponential in the input encoding size. The Lipschitz-based grid reduction of~\cite{DHKW08} can tolerate a large denominator because its integer solver depends polynomially on the denominator's bit length; explicit cell enumeration requires polynomially many cells.

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the algorithm. No local approximation to the objective is optimized. Instead, reflection controls the error at a global optimum, which is enough to justify the final selection. Section~\ref{sec:cover} proves this estimate, Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} gives the algorithm and its bit complexity, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses extensions and related decomposition methods.

More broadly, the method follows a decompose--solve--select pattern: a local error bound on the cell containing an optimum transfers to the best returned candidate. For polynomial optimization, this permits other cell decompositions or tractable local models when suitable error estimates are available.

The decomposition also connects to the geometric subdivision of Papadimitriou and Yannakakis~\cite{PY} and its use for affine slicing in Hildebrand, Weismantel, and Zemmer~\cite[Section~2]{HWZ}. Applying multiplicative bins to the slacks $s_i(\mathbf{x})=b_i-\mathbf a_i^\top\mathbf{x}$ gives the same relative-width property as our refined dyadic intervals: a bin $[a,(1+\delta)a]$ has width at most $\delta a$. Points in a common block therefore extend to feasible progressions of length $1/\delta$, with zero slacks handled separately. As in the proof of~\cite[Theorem~2.1]{HWZ}, the bins are pulled back to an arrangement in the original variable space $\R^d$; enumerating all combinations in the $m$-dimensional slack space would lose the fixed-dimensional complexity bound. Unlike the sliceable-objective framework of~\cite{HWZ}, our approximation argument uses Markov's inequality and measures error relative to the objective range.

To state the complexity bound precisely, we fix the following encoding convention. Vectors are columns and are written in bold. For an integer $u$, its \emph{binary encoding size} is $\langle u\rangle=1+\lceil\log_2(1+|u|)\rceil$, counting a sign bit and the binary magnitude bits. We use \emph{encoding size}, \emph{bit length}, or simply \emph{size} for storage measured in bits when the context is clear; \emph{magnitude} refers to the numerical absolute value. A rational number $u/v$ is stored in lowest terms with $v>0$, with size $\langle u/v\rangle=\langle u\rangle+\langle v\rangle$. For matrices, vectors, and lists, $\langle\cdot\rangle$ is the sum of the entry sizes together with the binary encoding sizes of the dimensions and list lengths. The polynomial is supplied explicitly as
\[
 f(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal A}c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\mathbf{x}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}},
 \qquad c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\in\Z,\quad \boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\Z_+^d,\quad |\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_1\le D.
\]
Following~\cite{DHKW08}, coefficients are encoded in binary and exponent vectors in unary: each exponent $\alpha_j$ is stored as $\alpha_j$ one-bits followed by a zero, using $\alpha_j+1$ bits. The input is therefore a bit string, although exponents use unary rather than positional binary notation. Accordingly, $\langle f\rangle$ includes the coefficient sizes and $\sum_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathcal A}\sum_{j=1}^d(\alpha_j+1)$, together with dimensions and list lengths. Write $\varphi=\langle A,\mathbf b,f,\eps\rangle$ for the total input encoding size. We set $D=\max\{1,\deg f\}$ from the nonzero monomials, taking $D=1$ for the zero polynomial; no separate degree input is needed. We retain $D$ explicitly in the running-time bound to show its contribution.

\begin{definition}[Range-relative FPTAS]\label{def:fptas}
Under the encoding convention above and for fixed dimension $d$, a range-relative fully polynomial-time approximation scheme is an algorithm that, for every rational $0<\eps\le1$, reports infeasibility when $P\cap\Z^d=\varnothing$, and otherwise returns $\mathbf{x}_{\eps}\in P\cap\Z^d$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:guarantee}
 0\le f(\mathbf{x}_{\eps})-f_{\min}\le\eps(f_{\max}-f_{\min}).
\end{equation}
Its running time is polynomial in the total input encoding size $\varphi$ and $1/\eps$. The polynomial may depend on the fixed dimension. This is the fully polynomial-time weak-approximation notion of~\cite{DHKW08}. The maximization definition uses $f_{\max}-f(\mathbf{x}_{\eps})$ on the left.
\end{definition}

All complexity statements below count bit operations. The function $\poly$ denotes a polynomial with an absolute exponent, independent of $d,D,\eps$; different occurrences may denote different polynomials. Logs are base two unless indicated otherwise.

\begin{theorem}[Polynomial approximation by reflecting cells]\label{thm:main}
Let $d\ge1$ and let $P=\{\mathbf{x}\in\R^d:A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf b\}$ be bounded, with $A\in\Z^{m\times d}$ and $\mathbf b\in\Z^m$, let $f\in\Z[x_1,\ldots,x_d]$, and put $D=\max\{1,\deg f\}$, with $D=1$ when $f\equiv0$. For rational $0<\eps\le1$, let $\varphi=\langle A,\mathbf b,f,\eps\rangle$ be the total input encoding size.
There is a deterministic algorithm that reports infeasibility or returns $\mathbf{x}_{\eps}\in P\cap\Z^d$ satisfying~\eqref{eq:guarantee}, in at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runtime-coarse}
 \left(\frac{\varphi D}{\eps}\right)^{O(d)}
\end{equation}
bit operations, with an absolute constant in the $O(d)$ exponent.
\end{theorem}

Since the input explicitly lists the $m$ inequalities in $d$ variables, $m,d\le\varphi$; their separate factors in the detailed running-time bound are therefore absorbed into the base $\varphi$ and the $O(d)$ exponent in~\eqref{eq:runtime-coarse}. The dependence on dimension remains explicit in that exponent. More precisely, for fixed $P,D,d$, the algorithm makes $O(\eps^{-d/2})$ integer-feasibility queries; the detailed running-time bound in the proof retains the factor $\max\{D^2,D/\sqrt\eps\}$.

Rational inequalities can be normalized to integral data in polynomial time, as detailed below. Rational objective coefficients can likewise be reduced to integer coefficients by clearing denominators in polynomial time; positive scaling preserves the approximation guarantee, as in~\cite[Section~5]{DHKW08}. The remaining construction parameters and the exact feasibility-query count are introduced in the proof. For fixed $d$, the bound is polynomial in $\varphi$ and $1/\eps$, hence gives an FPTAS in Definition~\ref{def:fptas}. The same explicit bound remains valid for binary-encoded exponents, but then its numerical dependence on $D$ does not assert an FPTAS in the sparse binary input size.

The following guarantees were established by De Loera et al.~\cite[Theorem~1.1]{DHKW06}\cite[Theorems~1(a) and~2(b)]{DHKW08}; we include them to explain how our integer algorithm fits their approximation framework. The first is the elementary conversion from range-relative error to nonnegative maximization. The second follows their integral-slice scaling, barycentric rounding, and reduction to integer optimization~\cite[Section~3]{DHKW08}. We modify the rounding estimate using Markov's inequality to obtain a coefficient-independent grid multiplier, and substitute Theorem~\ref{thm:main} for their integer subroutine.

\begin{corollary}[Nonnegative maximization {\cite[Theorem~1.1]{DHKW06}}]\label{cor:max}
Under the same assumptions and encoding conventions, if $f\ge0$ on $P\cap\Z^d$, choosing the largest objective value among the representatives yields
\[
 f(\mathbf{x}_{\eps})\ge(1-\eps)f_{\max}.
\]
Thus the construction gives a multiplicative FPTAS for nonnegative maximization under the encoding convention of Definition~\ref{def:fptas}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{corollary}[Mixed-integer approximation {\cite[Theorems~1(a) and~2(b)]{DHKW08}}]\label{cor:mixed}
Fix the total dimension $d=d_c+d_i$. Let $P=\{\mathbf{x}\in\R^d:A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf b\}$ be a bounded rational polyhedron and $f\in\Z[x_1,\ldots,x_d]$. Under the same encoding convention, for rational $0<\eps\le1$ there is an algorithm, polynomial in $\varphi=\langle A,\mathbf b,f,\eps\rangle$ and $1/\eps$, that reports infeasibility or returns
\[
 \mathbf{x}_{\eps}\in P\cap(\Q^{d_c}\times\Z^{d_i}),\qquad
 0\le f(\mathbf{x}_{\eps})-f_{\min}\le\eps(f_{\max}-f_{\min}),
\]
where the extrema are over $P\cap(\R^{d_c}\times\Z^{d_i})$. For nonnegative $f$ on this set, a multiplicative FPTAS for maximization is obtained as well. The running time is at most
\begin{equation}\label{eq:mixed-runtime}
 \left(\frac{\varphi D}{\eps}\right)^{O(d(d_c+1))}
\end{equation}
bit operations, with an absolute constant in the exponent.
\end{corollary}

\paragraph{Comparison with prior algorithms.}

The rational generating functions in these algorithms build on Barvinok's lattice-point counting method~\cite{Barvinok}. The guarantee in~\eqref{eq:guarantee} is already provided by De Loera, Hemmecke, K\"oppe, and Weismantel~\cite{DHKW06,DHKW08}, whose generating-function algorithms run in time polynomial in the input length, numerical degree, and $1/\eps$ for fixed total dimension. Their integer nonnegative-maximization subroutine computes weighted lattice sums for $f^q$, with $q=O(1+\eps^{-1}\log N)$ and $N=|P\cap\Z^d|$; solution recovery uses further weighted sums on restricted polytopes~\cite[Section~3]{DHKW06}. Thus the work includes both Barvinok cone decomposition and subsequent symbolic calculations. A standard bound for the underlying lattice-point counting algorithm is $\langle A,\mathbf b\rangle^{O(d\log(d+1))}$~\cite[Theorem~2.3]{PakPanova}, whereas~\eqref{eq:runtime-coarse} gives $\varphi^{O(d)}$ for our entire algorithm when $D$ and $\eps$ are fixed. This compares available upper bounds, not a lower bound on their algorithm; the counting bound alone does not describe their full FPTAS. Their published analysis does not make all dimension-dependent exponents in the weighted-summation cost explicit. Our construction instead gives the explicit bound~\eqref{eq:runtime-coarse} through cell enumeration, integer linear feasibility, and exact polynomial evaluation, and produces a representative set independent of the objective coefficients. These differences do not establish practical superiority: our cell count can be large, while generating-function methods can benefit from small cone decompositions.

Del Pia's construction shares the partition-and-solve structure, but uses different geometry~\cite[Sections~3--6]{DelPia}. After transforming the problem to spherical form, the aligned-vector case partitions the $k$ quadratic coordinates into uniform boxes and minimizes an affine underestimator on each box by MILP; the remaining cases use flatness-based recursion. It does not use geometric slack bins. Our representatives instead require only feasibility because the reflected sample at an integer minimizer controls the first-order contribution.

For fixed quadratic rank $k$ and integer dimension $p$, Del Pia~\cite[Proposition~3 and Section~6.2]{DelPia} uses $O_{k,p}(1+\eps^{-k/2})$ mixed-integer linear optimization subproblems, with polynomial bit overhead for fixed $k,p$, while allowing variable continuous dimension. For our pure-integer algorithm, with fixed $P,D,d$, the counts proved in Section~\ref{sec:algorithm} are
\[
 O(\eps^{-d/2})\ \text{integer feasibility calls},\qquad
 O(\eps^{-(d+1)/2})\ \text{enumeration LP tests}.
\]
The per-test arithmetic has polynomial dependence on the encoding length $\langle\eps\rangle$. The feasibility-call exponent matches $k/2$ when $k=d$, but the enumeration overhead remains and the rank-sensitive bound for $k<d$ is not recovered. Our construction instead applies to arbitrary degree and supplies one objective-independent representative set. Its dimension-dependent factor includes both the constraint normals and right-hand sides through their binary encoding lengths; the displayed bound is not a fixed-parameter tractability claim in $d$.

\section{Reflecting cells and approximation}\label{sec:cover}

The proof separates the geometry of the feasible set from the behavior of the objective. We first partition the integer points into cells whose pairs extend to long feasible progressions. We then restrict the polynomial to such a progression through a global minimizer. The reflected point rules out a large first-order improvement in the backward direction, and a second-difference estimate controls the error of the forward representative. Neither the minimizer nor the objective range needs to be known by the algorithm.

The construction begins by making the slack gap explicit. If the input inequalities are rational, multiply each row by the product of the positive denominators in that row. This gives integral $A,\mathbf b$ in polynomial time, with total encoding size polynomial in the original input size $\varphi$. Each normalized entry has bit length $O(\varphi)$, since a row product has bit length at most the sum of its denominator bit lengths. The detailed bounds below refer to these normalized data; substituting their polynomial encoding bounds gives a bound in the original input length. No coefficient magnitude appears as a polynomial-time parameter.

Explicit equalities can be represented by pairs of inequalities. Lower-dimensional polytopes are allowed; no full-dimensionality assumption or integer change of coordinates is needed. Continuous emptiness and boundedness are checkable by rational linear programming. Boundedness is a hypothesis of the approximation theorem; an unbounded polyhedron is outside its scope.

For the remainder of the construction, $A,\mathbf b$ denote the normalized integral data; $\varphi$ continues to denote the original input size. To track the construction explicitly, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tau}
 \tau=\left\lceil\log_2\bigl(2+\max\{\|A\|_{\max},\|\mathbf b\|_\infty\}\bigr)\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
Here $\|A\|_{\max}$ is the largest absolute matrix entry, so $\tau$ bounds the coefficient bit lengths up to a constant. The remaining parameters have the following roles: $\Phi$ gives the common magnitude bound $2^\Phi$ for coordinates and shifted slacks and determines how many intervals $[2^k,2^{k+1}]$ are needed below, $\ell$ is the refinement, and $H$ bounds the number of hyperplanes. Set
\begin{align}
 \Phi&=(d+1)\bigl(\tau+\lceil\log_2(d+1)\rceil\bigr)+4,\label{eq:Phi}\\
 \ell&=\max\{D^2,\lceil D/\sqrt\eps\rceil\},\qquad H=m((\Phi+2)\ell+1),\label{eq:HT}\\
 R_d(H)&=\sum_{j=0}^{d}\binom Hj\le(1+H)^d.\label{eq:F}
\end{align}

Following the outward perturbation in~\cite[proof of Theorem~2.1, p.~29]{CHKM}, define
\[
\begin{gathered}
 \widetilde P=\{\mathbf{x}:A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf b+\tfrac12\mathbf1\},\\
 s_i(\mathbf{x})=b_i+\tfrac12-\mathbf a_i^\top\mathbf{x}.
\end{gathered}
\]
Since $A,\mathbf b$ are integral, $\widetilde P\cap\Z^d=P\cap\Z^d$. Every integer point of $P$ has $s_i\ge1/2$. We will extrapolate real segments in $\widetilde P$ and use integrality to return their integer samples to $P$. This shift is applied only to the pure-integer problem, including the scaled integer problem in the mixed-integer reduction.

The following standard determinant bounds specialize the polyhedral encoding estimates discussed in~\cite[Chapter~10]{Schrijver}. We include the short argument to record the explicit common exponent $\Phi$ used below.

\begin{lemma}[Coordinates and slacks]\label{lem:size}
If $P\ne\varnothing$, every $\mathbf{x}\in P$ satisfies $|x_j|<2^\Phi$ for every $j$. Every shifted slack satisfies $1/2\le s_i(\mathbf{x})<2^\Phi$, with $\Phi$ defined in~\eqref{eq:Phi}.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
A vertex of a bounded polyhedron in $\R^d$ has $d$ linearly independent active constraint normals, including when the polyhedron is lower-dimensional. Cramer's rule expresses each vertex coordinate as a ratio of two integral determinants of order $d$. The nonzero denominator has absolute value at least one. Since every entry of $A,\mathbf{b}$ has absolute value less than $2^\tau$, the determinant expansion gives
\[
 |x_j|\le d!2^{d\tau}
 \le 2^{d(\tau+\lceil\log_2(d+1)\rceil)}<2^\Phi
\]
for every vertex. The same bound holds throughout $P$, since every point is a convex combination of vertices. Feasibility gives shifted slacks at least $1/2$; substituting the sharper coordinate estimate above gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 s_i(\mathbf{x})&\le\tfrac12+ |b_i|+\sum_{j=1}^d|a_{ij}|\,|x_j|\\
 &\le\tfrac12+ 2^{(d+1)(\tau+\lceil\log_2(d+1)\rceil)+1}<2^\Phi.
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proof}

The shifted slacks remove the zero-slack boundary case, even when $P$ is lower-dimensional. The dyadic construction in~\cite[Theorem~2.2]{AH}, tracing back to~\cite{CHKM}, groups comparable positive slacks; we refine these groups to obtain long progressions. Figure~\ref{fig:pentagon-cells} illustrates the shifted construction.

\begin{definition}[Closed refined cells]\label{def:thresholds}
For integers $\Phi,\ell\ge1$, let
\begin{equation}\label{eq:thresholds}
\begin{split}
\mathcal B_{\Phi,\ell}=\{2^\Phi\}\cup\\
 \left\{2^k\left(1+\frac j\ell\right):-2\le k<\Phi,\ 0\le j<\ell\right\}.
\end{split}
\end{equation}
There are $(\Phi+2)\ell+1$ thresholds, starting at $1/4$. Set
\[
 Q=\{\mathbf{x}:1/4\le s_i(\mathbf{x})\le2^\Phi\ (1\le i\le m)\}.
\]
Subdivide $Q$ by the hyperplanes $s_i(\mathbf{x})=a$, $a\in\mathcal B_{\Phi,\ell}$. The family $\mathcal C$ consists of the closures of the resulting full-dimensional chambers. If $Q$ has empty interior, set $\mathcal C=\varnothing$.
\end{definition}

Every integer point of $P$ lies in the interior of $Q$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:size}, and therefore belongs to a closed cell. The cells may overlap on subdivision hyperplanes, but no separate lower-dimensional faces are needed. Conversely, $Q\subseteq\widetilde P$, so every integer point in a cell belongs to $P$. These statements also justify declaring integer infeasibility when $Q$ has empty interior. The set $Q$ is bounded: its recession directions satisfy $A\mathbf v=0$, which forces $\mathbf v=0$ since $P$ is nonempty and bounded.

If $a<b$ are successive thresholds, then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:width}
 b-a\le a/\ell.
\end{equation}
Indeed, on $[2^k,2^{k+1}]$ the spacing is $2^k/\ell$ and $a\ge2^k$. The same argument applies to the two additional scales below one.

\begin{lemma}[Refined displacement cover]\label{lem:reflection}
Each $C\in\mathcal C$ is described by $a_i\le s_i(\mathbf{x})\le c_i$ for successive thresholds $a_i<c_i$. Set
\[
 D_C=\{\mathbf v\in\R^d:|\mathbf a_i^\top\mathbf v|\le a_i\ (1\le i\le m)\}.
\]
Then $\ell(C-C)\subseteq D_C$ and $C\pm D_C\subseteq\widetilde P$. In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:long}
 \mathbf{x}+k(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x})\in P\cap\Z^d
 \quad(\mathbf{x},\mathbf y\in C\cap\Z^d,\ k\in\Z,\ |k|\le\ell).
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
For $\mathbf{x},\mathbf y\in C$, the weak interval bounds give
$|s_i(\mathbf y)-s_i(\mathbf{x})|\le c_i-a_i\le a_i/\ell$ and $s_i(\mathbf{x})\ge a_i$. These prove both inclusions row by row. Since $D_C$ is convex and symmetric, the whole real segment $\mathbf{x}+[-\ell,\ell](\mathbf y-\mathbf{x})$ lies in $\widetilde P$. If $\mathbf{x},\mathbf y$ and $k$ are integral, its sample is integral and hence belongs to $P$. Boundedness of $D_C$ follows from $A\mathbf v=0\Rightarrow\mathbf v=0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Relative slacks characterize extrapolation]\label{rem:slack-characterization}
Let $\mathbf{x},\mathbf y\in\widetilde P$, set $\mathbf v=\mathbf y-\mathbf{x}$, and let $\ell>0$. The segments $\mathbf{x}+[-\ell,\ell]\mathbf v$ and $\mathbf y+[-\ell,\ell]\mathbf v$, centered at $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf y$ respectively, both lie in $\widetilde P$ if and only if, for every row $i$,
\[
 \ell\,|s_i(\mathbf y)-s_i(\mathbf{x})|
 \le\min\{s_i(\mathbf{x}),s_i(\mathbf y)\}.
\]
Indeed, the minimum slack along the first segment is $s_i(\mathbf{x})-\ell|s_i(\mathbf y)-s_i(\mathbf{x})|$, and similarly for the second. For positive slacks the condition is equivalently
\[
 \frac{\max\{s_i(\mathbf{x}),s_i(\mathbf y)\}}
 {\min\{s_i(\mathbf{x}),s_i(\mathbf y)\}}\le1+\frac1\ell;
\]
if either slack is zero, both must be zero. Our cells have positive shifted slacks, so this last case never arises. Thus multiplicative slack control is exactly the geometry needed for extrapolation about both points. Refined dyadic intervals enforce this condition with short rational descriptions, and integrality limits the number of relevant scales. The condition is independent of the objective: Proposition~\ref{prop:extrema} turns the resulting cover into a simultaneous approximation guarantee for every polynomial of degree at most $D$.
\end{remark}

The integer progression property is the geometric input to approximation. Closed chambers also give weak linear descriptions directly; integer rounding below only removes threshold denominators.

\begin{lemma}[Number, encoding, and enumeration of cells]\label{lem:arrangement}
Let $H=m((\Phi+2)\ell+1)$ and set
\[
 B=\tau+\Phi+\lceil\log_2(\ell+1)\rceil+10.
\]
There are at most $R_d(H)$ closed cells. Each has at most $2m$ weak slack inequalities with coefficient bit length $O(B)$. They can be enumerated deterministically in
\begin{equation}\label{eq:arrangement-time}
 (H+1)R_d(H)\,\poly(\langle A,\mathbf b\rangle+d+m+B)
\end{equation}
bit operations. Each cell has a closed rational subset $\widehat C$ with exactly the same integer points, described by at most $2m$ inequalities of coefficient bit length $O(\tau+\Phi+1)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The standard chamber bound for $H$ hyperplanes in $\R^d$ is $\sum_{j=0}^d\binom Hj$; see~\cite{Stanley}. Indeed, insertion of one hyperplane creates at most as many new chambers as the induced arrangement on that hyperplane, giving the recurrence $R_d(H)=R_d(H-1)+R_{d-1}(H-1)$ for the upper bound. Restriction to the convex set $Q$ cannot split a chamber, so the same bound applies here.

Each threshold has denominator dividing $4\ell$ and numerator of bit length $O(\Phi+\log(\ell+1))$. Clearing this denominator and the half-unit shift gives the stated coefficient bound.

Start with $Q$ and insert the threshold hyperplanes one at a time. Split each current closed cell into its two closed halfspace intersections, keeping only those with nonempty interior. Their closures still cover the parent: if the hyperplane cuts its interior, both pieces have interior; otherwise one piece is the parent. Thus no separate equality branch is needed. At most $R_d(H)$ cells occur at any stage. Bounds from the same row compress to the strongest lower and upper bounds, leaving at most $2m$ inequalities per cell.

For a system $u_j^\top\mathbf{x}\le v_j$ with nonzero normals, test nonempty interior by maximizing $\delta$ subject to $u_j^\top\mathbf{x}\le v_j-\delta$ and $0\le\delta\le1$. The optimum is positive exactly when the system has interior. Zero rows were removed in preprocessing. These are rational LPs in $d+1$ variables, and there are $O((H+1)R_d(H))$ tests, including the initial test for $Q$.

Finally, replace $a\le s_i(\mathbf{x})\le c$ by
\[
 \lceil a-\tfrac12\rceil\le b_i-\mathbf a_i^\top\mathbf{x}
 \le\lfloor c-\tfrac12\rfloor.
\]
The middle expression is integral on $\Z^d$, so this defines $\widehat C\subseteq C$ with the same integer points. Its right-hand sides have $O(\tau+\Phi+1)$ bits. All rounding is exact. Cells can overlap, so representatives may be repeated; this does not affect feasibility or selection.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Parallel families]
If row $i$ contributes $q_i$ distinct threshold hyperplanes, the count can be sharpened to
\[
 |\mathcal C|\le
 \sum_{\substack{I\subseteq\{1,\ldots,m\}\\|I|\le d}}
 \prod_{i\in I}q_i
 \le\sum_{j=0}^d\binom mj((\Phi+2)\ell+1)^j.
\]
The insertion argument counts intersections involving at most one hyperplane from each parallel family; further dependencies can only reduce the count. This improves the bound without changing its worst-case $\ell^d$ dependence. We retain $R_d(H)$ for simplicity. The boundary-vertex count in~\cite[pp.~30--31]{CHKM} bounds integer-hull vertices, not all reflecting cells, and therefore does not directly replace this count.
\end{remark}

We now turn to the objective. A polynomial can vary substantially between consecutive integer samples, so bounded sample values do not immediately bound its derivatives. Markov's inequality resolves this in two steps: first it controls the polynomial between the samples, and then symmetrization reduces the needed second difference to a first difference of a lower-degree polynomial. At an integer minimizer, the backward sample supplies the additional inequality needed to turn that derivative bound into an approximation guarantee. This avoids imposing a continuous stationarity condition or approximating the objective uniformly on every cell.

The following elementary consequence of the classical Markov inequality combines the standard sample-to-supremum argument used in polynomial norming meshes (see~\cite[proof of Proposition~2]{PV}) with an even-part bound for a centered second difference. We include the short proof for the discrete-minimum formulation needed here.

\begin{lemma}[Approximation at a discrete minimum]\label{lem:markov}
Let $D\ge1$ and $\ell\ge D^2$ be integers, and let $p\in\R[t]$ have degree at most $D$. Suppose
\[
 a\le p(k)\le b\quad(k=-\ell,\ldots,\ell),\qquad p(-1)\ge p(0).
\]
Then
\[
 p(1)-p(0)\le \frac{D^2}{\ell^2}(b-a).
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Set $q=p-(a+b)/2$ and $W=\|q\|_{\infty,[-\ell,\ell]}$. The scaled classical Markov inequality gives $\|q'\|_\infty\le D^2W/\ell$; see~\cite[Theorem~5.1.8]{BorweinErdelyi}. Every point of $[-\ell,\ell]$ lies within $1/2$ of a sampled integer, so
\[
 W\le \frac{b-a}{2}+\frac{D^2}{2\ell}W\le\frac{b-a+W}{2},
 \qquad W\le b-a.
\]
Let $n=\lfloor D/2\rfloor$. The even part of $q$ has the form
\[
 e(t)=\frac{q(t)+q(-t)}2=h(t^2),\qquad \deg h\le n.
\]
Thus $\|h\|_{\infty,[0,\ell^2]}\le W$. Markov's inequality on $[0,\ell^2]$ gives $\|h'\|_\infty\le 2n^2W/\ell^2$. Since $\ell\ge1$, the mean value bound on $[0,1]$ and $p(-1)\ge p(0)$ yield
\[
\begin{aligned}
 p(1)-p(0)&\le p(1)+p(-1)-2p(0)\\
 &=2(h(1)-h(0))\\
 &\le\frac{4\lfloor D/2\rfloor^2}{\ell^2}(b-a)
 \le\frac{D^2}{\ell^2}(b-a).
\end{aligned}
\]
For $D=1$, $h$ is constant and the bound is zero. The argument also covers $a=b$.
\end{proof}

The estimate is needed only on the cell containing a global minimizer, and holds for any representative of that cell. Applying it also to $-f$ gives the following simultaneous approximation of both extrema. The general conversion from a polynomial norming inequality to range-relative optimization is given in~\cite[Proposition~1]{PV}; here we use the discrete-minimum estimate directly. The reflecting geometry supplies the feasible integer samples needed here.

\begin{proposition}[Representatives approximate extrema]\label{prop:extrema}
Choose one integer representative from each integer-feasible closed cell, forming $Y$. If $P\cap\Z^d\ne\varnothing$ and $\ell\ge D^2$, then every real polynomial $f$ of degree at most $D$ satisfies
\[
\begin{aligned}
 0&\le\min_Y f-\min_{P\cap\Z^d} f\le\frac{D^2}{\ell^2}(f_{\max}-f_{\min}),\\
 0&\le\max_{P\cap\Z^d} f-\max_Y f\le\frac{D^2}{\ell^2}(f_{\max}-f_{\min}).
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $\mathbf{x}_{\min}$ minimize $f$ on $P\cap\Z^d$, and let $\mathbf{y}$ represent a cell containing it. Put $p(t)=f(\mathbf{x}_{\min}+t(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}_{\min}))$. Lemma~\ref{lem:reflection} makes all integer samples with $|k|\le \ell$ feasible, so they lie between $f_{\min}$ and $f_{\max}$. Minimality gives $p(-1)\ge p(0)$. Lemma~\ref{lem:markov} now bounds $f(\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x}_{\min})$. Apply the same argument to $-f$ for the maximum.
\end{proof}

\section{Algorithm and complexity}\label{sec:algorithm}

The preceding results lead directly to Algorithm~\ref{alg:sampling}: enumerate the refined cells, find one integer point in each nonempty cell, and compare the objective values at those points. The geometric work is independent of the objective coefficients. This is also why the representatives can be reused for different polynomials of degree at most $D$. For this purpose, the algorithm accepts any prescribed degree bound $D\ge\max\{1,\deg f\}$; Theorem~\ref{thm:main} uses the bound computed from $f$. A constant objective requires only one integer feasibility query; dimension zero is similarly immediate. Representatives can be processed as a stream, or saved as a universal set $Y$ for subsequent objectives.

\begin{proof}[Correctness in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}]
Every integer-feasible cell contributes a feasible representative, and the closed cells cover $P\cap\Z^d$. Thus $Y=\varnothing$ if and only if $P\cap\Z^d=\varnothing$. Otherwise, Proposition~\ref{prop:extrema} and $\ell=\max\{D^2,\lceil D/\sqrt\eps\rceil\}$ give
\[
 0\le\min_Y f-f_{\min}\le \frac{D^2}{\ell^2}(f_{\max}-f_{\min})\le\eps (f_{\max}-f_{\min}).
\]
The algorithm returns a minimizer on $Y$. The cover and representatives depend only on $P,D,\eps$, so the same set works for every polynomial of degree at most $D$.
\end{proof}

It remains to account for bit operations. There are three costs: constructing the arrangement, producing integer representatives, and evaluating the objective. The distinction between the number of cells and the sizes of their descriptions is important: refinement increases the former polynomially in $1/\sqrt\eps$, whereas it increases the threshold bit lengths only logarithmically.
By Lemma~\ref{lem:arrangement}, chamber enumeration costs~\eqref{eq:arrangement-time}. The bit length of $\ell$ is $O(\log(D+1)+\langle\eps\rangle)$; compute the least positive integer $k$ satisfying $\eps k^2\ge D^2$ by binary search with rational comparisons, and set $\ell=\max\{D^2,k\}$. Generating all thresholds uses $O(\Phi\ell)$ arithmetic operations on integers with $O(\Phi+\log(\ell+1))$ bits and is included in the arrangement bound when $m\ge1$. A nonempty bounded polyhedron in positive dimension cannot be specified by zero inequalities.

Once a cell has been enumerated, its integer feasibility query has a description whose coefficient sizes do not grow with the refinement parameter.
Each of at most $R_d(H)$ queries has $d$ integer variables and a total encoding size
\begin{equation}\label{eq:ilp-size}
 S_C=O\!\left(m(d+1)(\tau+\Phi+1)+d+m\right).
\end{equation}
Fixed-dimensional integer linear feasibility with witness production takes $2^{O(d\log(d+1))}S_C^{O(1)}$ bit operations using Kannan's algorithm~\cite{Kannan}, improving the dimension dependence of Lenstra's algorithm~\cite{Lenstra}. Every integer point returned from a shifted cell belongs to the original bounded polytope. Accordingly, every returned coordinate has at most $\Phi+O(1)$ bits by Lemma~\ref{lem:size}; its bit length grows logarithmically with its magnitude. Notice also that~\eqref{eq:ilp-size} has no dependence on $\ell$ other than the number of cells, because the threshold inequalities have already been rounded to integer slack bounds.

Finally, the coordinate bound ensures that exact objective evaluation also has polynomial bit cost, even though feasible coordinates can be numerically large.
The integer coefficients of $f$ have bit length at most $\langle f\rangle$. If $\mathbf{x}\in P\cap\Z^d$, every monomial has absolute value at most $2^{D\Phi}$. Each value of $f(\mathbf{x})$, and the intermediates obtained by evaluating monomials and summing them, has $O(\langle f\rangle+D\Phi+\log(|\mathcal A|+1))$ bits. Repeated squaring evaluates the explicit exponent vectors in a number of arithmetic operations polynomial in $\langle f\rangle+d+\log(D+1)$. Even schoolbook integer arithmetic therefore gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eval}
 \poly(\langle f\rangle+d+D\Phi)
\end{equation}
bit operations per representative. The case of the zero polynomial is immediate. Returning an exact integer objective value also fits this bound. No unit-cost assumption on rational arithmetic is used.

Combining these three costs gives the explicit bound
\begin{equation}\label{eq:runtime-detailed}
 \left((H+1)R_d(H)+2^{O(d\log(d+1))}R_d(H)\right)
 \poly\!\left(\varphi+d+m+\Phi+\log(\ell+1)+D\Phi\right).
\end{equation}
The polynomial here has an absolute exponent. Since $\Phi=O(d(\tau+\log(d+1)))$ and $\Lambda\le\ell\le2\Lambda$ for $\Lambda=\max\{D^2,D/\sqrt\eps\}$, the more detailed parameter bound is
\[
 2^{O(d\log(d+1))}
 \left(1+md(\tau+\log(d+1))\Lambda\right)^{d+1}
 \poly\!\left(\varphi+Dd(\tau+\log(d+1))\right).
\]
For the integral input in Theorem~\ref{thm:main}, $\tau=O(\varphi)$ and $d\le\varphi$. Consequently $\tau+\log(d+1)=O(\varphi)$, and the factor $d^{d+1}$ is absorbed into $2^{O(d\log(d+1))}$. The polynomial overhead is bounded by $\poly(\varphi,D)$. Since $m,\varphi,D\ge1$ and $\eps\le1$, the additive one can also be absorbed, giving
\[
 2^{O(d\log(d+1))}
 \left(m\varphi\Lambda\right)^{d+1}
 \poly(\varphi,D).
\]
Finally, $m,d\le\varphi$ allows the dimension factor and the polynomial overhead to be absorbed into $(\varphi D/\eps)^{O(d)}$, proving~\eqref{eq:runtime-coarse}. The dependence on the objective encoding $\langle f\rangle$ enters through exact evaluation in~\eqref{eq:eval}; the cell construction is independent of the objective coefficients.
The factor $2^{O(d\log(d+1))}$ comes from integer linear feasibility. The exponent $d+1$ includes the extra factor from incremental chamber enumeration. For fixed $d$, normalization and all encoding terms are polynomial in the original $\varphi$ and numerical $D$; the accuracy encoding also bounds $\log(\ell+1)$ up to a term $O(\log(D+1))$. This proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. The algorithm makes at most $R_d(H)$ integer feasibility queries; for fixed $P,D,d$, this is $O(\eps^{-d/2})$.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:max}]
Applying Proposition~\ref{prop:extrema} to $-f$ gives $f_{\max}-f(\mathbf{x}_{\eps})\le\eps (f_{\max}-f_{\min})$. Nonnegativity on $P\cap\Z^d$ implies $f_{\max}-f_{\min}\le f_{\max}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:mixed}]
If $d_c=0$, apply Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Otherwise set $r=d_c$ and $S=P\cap(\R^r\times\Z^{d_i})$. By~\cite[Lemma~9]{DHKW08}, an integer $\Delta>0$ of polynomial encoding size in fixed dimension can be computed such that every nonempty slice $\Delta P_{\mathbf z}$ is integral. We modify the barycentric rounding in~\cite[Lemma~10]{DHKW08} and use Markov's inequality instead of a coefficient-based Lipschitz estimate. Set
\[
 k=\left\lceil\frac{4D^2r(r+1)}{\eps}\right\rceil,
 \qquad Q=k\Delta,\qquad t=\frac{r(r+1)}k.
\]
For any $\mathbf x\in P_{\mathbf z}$, Carath\'eodory's theorem gives $\mathbf x=\sum_{i=0}^r\lambda_i\mathbf v_i$ for slice vertices $\mathbf v_i\in\Delta^{-1}\Z^r$ and convex weights, indexed so that $\lambda_0\ge1/(r+1)$. Put
\[
 \mu_i=\frac{\lceil k\lambda_i\rceil}{k}\ (i\ge1),
 \qquad \mu_0=1-\sum_{i=1}^r\mu_i.
\]
For $i\ge1$, $\lambda_i\le\mu_i\le\lambda_i+1/k$, while
$\mu_0\ge\lambda_0-r/k\ge(1-t)\lambda_0$. Since $0<t\le1$, all weights are nonnegative and $\mu_i\ge(1-t)\lambda_i$. Therefore
\[
 \mathbf y=\sum_{i=0}^r\mu_i\mathbf v_i
 =(1-t)\mathbf x+t\mathbf w\in P_{\mathbf z}\cap Q^{-1}\Z^r
\]
for some $\mathbf w\in P_{\mathbf z}$: its convex weights are $(\mu_i-(1-t)\lambda_i)/t$.

Write $R=f_{\max}-f_{\min}$ over $S$. The polynomial
$p(u)=f((1-u)\mathbf x+u\mathbf w,\mathbf z)-f_{\min}$ has degree at most $D$ and lies in $[0,R]$ on $[0,1]$. Markov's inequality gives
\[
 |f(\mathbf y,\mathbf z)-f(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)|
 \le2D^2tR\le\eps R/2.
\]
Thus the grid preserves nonemptiness and approximates the minimum within $\eps R/2$, including when $R=0$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:main} on the grid with accuracy $\eps/2$. Its objective range is at most $R$, so the total error is at most $\eps R$.

Substitute $\mathbf x=\mathbf u/Q$ and use the integer polynomial $g(\mathbf u,\mathbf z)=Q^D f(\mathbf u/Q,\mathbf z)$. Its degree and support are unchanged, and coefficient bit lengths increase by $O(D\log(Q+1))$. Since
\[
 \log Q=\log\Delta+
 O\!\left(\log(D+1)+\log(r+1)+\log(1/\eps)\right),
\]
positive scaling preserves the guarantee; negation and nonnegativity give the maximization claim. For explicit complexity, normalize the inequalities to integers and let $\tau$ bound their coefficient bit lengths. DHKW construct $\Delta$ as the least common multiple of nonzero absolute determinants of $r$-row bases of the continuous-variable matrix~\cite[proof of Lemma~9]{DHKW08}. Hadamard's inequality gives
\[
 \log\Delta=O\!\left(\binom mr r(\tau+\log(r+1))\right).
\]
Enumerating these bases and computing their least common multiple takes $\varphi^{O(r+1)}$ bit operations; normalization changes encoding size only polynomially. The scaled input has size at most $\varphi^{O(r+1)}$ under our unary-exponent convention. Applying Theorem~\ref{thm:main} proves~\eqref{eq:mixed-runtime}. For binary exponents, the extra polynomial dependence on $D$ is likewise absorbed into the displayed bound.

The new grid multiplier avoids the coefficient Lipschitz constant and positive range-gap bound in DHKW's reduction. This sharpens the sufficient grid resolution, but does not alone establish a better overall asymptotic runtime: the integer solvers depend on $\log Q$, and the common denominator $\Delta$ remains.
\end{proof}

\section{Discussion and extensions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Symmetrization gives two refinement regimes: $\ell=D^2$ when $\eps\ge D^{-2}$, and $\ell=\lceil D/\sqrt\eps\rceil$ when $\eps\le D^{-2}$. In the latter regime, replacing the earlier twice-differentiated Markov estimate improves the refinement-dependent factors in the cell-count and enumeration bounds by $D^d$ and $D^{d+1}$, respectively, up to ceiling effects. These are improvements in upper bounds, not guaranteed instance-by-instance speedups; the accuracy exponents are unchanged.

The accuracy exponent for objective-independent representative sets has a simple cardinality obstruction. On $P_N=[0,N]^d$, suppose $Y\subseteq P_N\cap\Z^d$ approximates all quadratic minima with range-relative accuracy $\eps$. For each integer center $\mathbf a$, apply this guarantee to $f_{\mathbf a}(\mathbf x)=\|\mathbf x-\mathbf a\|_2^2$. Its minimum is zero and its range is at most $dN^2$, so some $\mathbf y\in Y$ lies within $N\sqrt{d\eps}$ of $\mathbf a$. Each representative covers at most $(2N\sqrt{d\eps}+1)^d$ integer centers, giving
\[
 |Y|\ge\frac{(N+1)^d}{(2N\sqrt{d\eps}+1)^d}.
\]
For integer $N\ge\eps^{-1/2}$ this is $\Omega_d(\eps^{-d/2})$. This lower bound concerns universal representative sets across a family of boxes, not arbitrary optimization algorithms or one fixed finite lattice set. It does not explain the additional $\eps^{-1/2}$ factor in our enumeration bound.

For any refinement $\ell\ge1$, the representatives include every vertex of the integer hull. Indeed, if such a vertex $\mathbf v$ shared a cell with a distinct integer point $\mathbf y$, reflection would make $2\mathbf v-\mathbf y$ feasible, contradicting extremality of $\mathbf v=(\mathbf y+(2\mathbf v-\mathbf y))/2$. Thus every linear objective is optimized exactly.

The main result concerns polynomial optimization: a refined reflecting cover and one integer-feasibility query per cell suffice for a range-relative FPTAS. Corollary~\ref{cor:mixed} supplies the mixed-integer extension through the established grid reduction. Table~\ref{tab:framework} places this construction alongside related decomposition and sampling methods; the common error-transfer principle is recorded in Appendix~\ref{app:comparison}.

For polynomial objectives with additional structure, one can replace feasibility by optimization of a local surrogate $h_C$. If $|f-h_C|\le\delta_C$ on the feasible part of a cell and a feasible candidate minimizes $h_C$ there within additive error $\eta_C$, then selecting the best candidate gives error at most $2\delta_{C^*}+\eta_{C^*}$, where $C^*$ contains a global minimizer. Affine, convex, or quadratic surrogates are useful when their local optimization problems are tractable and their errors can be bounded relative to the global objective range. This includes the frozen-weight idea for sliceable objectives~\cite{HWZ} and affine models for quadratics~\cite{DelPia}. An FPTAS still requires polynomial bounds on the cover construction, local computations, and encoding sizes; the transfer inequality alone does not supply them.

A further direction is to exploit a supplied factorization $f(\mathbf{x})=g(B\mathbf{x})+\mathbf c^\top\mathbf{x}$, where $B\in\Q^{k\times d}$ has few rows. Local affine optimization could handle the remaining directions without refinement, suggesting an accuracy dependence governed by $k$ rather than $d$, as in the quadratic setting of Del Pia~\cite{DelPia}. For higher-degree polynomials, this would require a cover whose size reflects the nonlinear dimension while preserving sufficient feasible samples to control the approximation error relative to the integer objective range. Even with fixed ambient dimension, replacing the current accuracy dependence by $\eps^{-O(k)}$ is not established by our analysis; merely changing the local oracle leaves the existing cell count unchanged.

The reflecting argument also extends to polynomial optimization over a bounded set $K$ whenever an efficiently constructible polynomial-size cover of $K\cap\Z^d$ supports the same feasible integer progressions and admits efficient integer-feasibility queries. An outer polyhedral relaxation alone is insufficient, because reflected points may leave $K$; a suitable relaxation with exactly the same integer points would suffice. General convex sets need not admit such a cover: at a boundary lattice point of a strictly convex set, the reflection property forces its cell to contain no other lattice point. Extensions beyond polyhedra may therefore require different local error estimates or stronger local optimization oracles. These are conditions for further generalization, rather than additional FPTAS results established here.

\section{A local comparison principle}\label{app:comparison}
Let $\mathcal X$ denote an abstract feasible set and let $F$ be the objective to minimize. For maximization use $F=-f$; for strictly positive objectives, $F=\log f$ or $F=-\log f$ expresses multiplicative guarantees additively. These transformations are analytical: candidates may be compared using the original objective, without computing logarithms. A cover procedure supplies finitely many comparison regions $C$, admissible output sets $R_C\subseteq\mathcal X$, and surrogate functions $h_C$ defined on $(C\cap\mathcal X)\cup R_C$. Usually $R_C=C\cap\mathcal X$, but allowing a larger output set accommodates dominance oracles and rounding.

Construct the finite cover and, for each region $C$, either certify $C\cap\mathcal X=\varnothing$ or return a feasible candidate $y_C\in R_C$ with
\[
 h_C(y_C)\le\inf_{x\in C\cap\mathcal X}h_C(x)+\eta_C.
\]
Report infeasibility if no candidate is returned; otherwise select a candidate with minimum $F$-value.


\begin{proposition}[Local comparison principle]\label{prop:local-comparison}
Suppose $\mathcal X\ne\varnothing$, $F$ attains its minimum at $x^*$, and the cover and local oracles in the procedure above satisfy their stated guarantees, with $\eta_C\ge0$. Suppose some $C^*\ni x^*$ satisfies, for every $y\in R_{C^*}$,
\[
 (F-h_{C^*})(y)-(F-h_{C^*})(x^*)\le\beta_{C^*}.
\]
Then the returned point satisfies
\[
 F(\widehat x)-F(x^*)\le\eta_{C^*}+\beta_{C^*}.
\]
If $\mathcal X=\varnothing$, the algorithm reports infeasibility.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The oracle cannot discard $C^*$, since it contains $x^*$. By selection of $\widehat x$,
\[
 F(\widehat x)-F(x^*)
 \le F(y_{C^*})-F(x^*)
 \le h_{C^*}(y_{C^*})-h_{C^*}(x^*)+\beta_{C^*}
 \le\eta_{C^*}+\beta_{C^*}.
\]
When $\mathcal X=\varnothing$, no feasible candidate can be returned and every region is discarded.
\end{proof}

The algorithm has cost
\[
 T_{\mathrm{cover}}+\sum_{C\in\mathcal C}
 \bigl(T_{\mathrm{local}}(C)+T_{\mathrm{compare}}(C)\bigr),
\]
where comparison includes objective evaluation. An FPTAS follows only when these costs and all constructed descriptions are polynomial in the input size and $1/\eps$, for the fixed structural parameters, and the error bound gives the requested guarantee. The scale $\eps(f_{\max}-f_{\min})$ or a logarithmic multiplicative error may be used in the proof without being known numerically to the algorithm. Establishing effective covers, local oracles, and error certificates remains the substantive problem in each application.

The new module \texttt{ScaledPolynomial.lean} constructs the integer polynomial with coefficient $c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}} Q^{D-\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_i}$, where $I$ indexes the continuous coordinates. Its first eight theorems prove the exponent bound, coefficient formula, unchanged monomial support and total degree for $Q\ne0$, and the evaluation identity $g(\mathbf u,\mathbf z)=Q^D f(\mathbf u/Q,\mathbf z)$. The declaration \texttt{integer\_\allowbreak representation} packages an actual integer-coefficient polynomial, its degree bound and the identity; these properties are conclusions, not hypotheses. Twelve further results verify inverse coordinate maps, the scaled-row identity, and a bijection between the scaled integer system and the compatible-grid system. They also prove the signed binary-length formula, the bound $\langle c'_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rangle\le\langle c_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\rangle+D\,\mathrm{size}(|Q|)$, and the corresponding summed coefficient-payload bound. Here $\mathrm{size}$ is binary magnitude length; Lean identifies the signed length with $1+\operatorname{clog}_2(1+|c|)$ using the natural ceiling logarithm. Six further results verify the scaled constraint lengths. Each matrix entry and right-hand side grows by at most $\mathrm{size}(|Q|)$ bits; the total dense constraint payload grows by at most $m(d+1)\mathrm{size}(|Q|)$. The same bound holds after adding unchanged dimension/list metadata. Twelve further results construct positive product denominators and integer entries for rational rows, including each right-hand side. They prove the real scaling identity and equivalence of the whole constraint system, with arbitrary additional domain restrictions. The denominator and entry lengths are bounded explicitly; a row with rational payload $L$ has cleared integer payload at most $L(L+2)$. These bounds are also summed across rows. Ten further results check the mixed-integer complexity calculation. \texttt{continuous\_basis\_count} and \texttt{continuous\_basis\_power\_bound} count candidate row bases. \texttt{integer\_basis\_determinant\_size} bounds integer determinant lengths using the checked Leibniz estimate. \texttt{basis\_lcm\_spec} proves positivity and divisibility of the actual finite least common multiple, and \texttt{basis\_lcm\_size} bounds its length. \texttt{mixed\_grid\_size} propagates this to $Q=k\Delta$. The results \texttt{mixed\_grid\_polynomial\_size} and \texttt{mixed\_scaled\_input\_size} check polynomial encoding absorption. Finally, \texttt{mixed\_runtime\_composition} and \texttt{mixed\_runtime\_exponent} derive an explicit constant multiple of $d(d_c+1)$ in the exponent from preprocessing and integer-solver bounds.
